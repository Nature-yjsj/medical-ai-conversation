## 3. Preserving bilateral renal function

- **Avoid nephrotoxins** when possible, including NSAIDs, aminoglycosides, and unnecessary iodinated contrast. Use hydration protocols when contrast is required.
- **Local therapy for kidney lesions.** For any persistent or growing renal masses, consider active surveillance, partial nephrectomy, or ablation before resorting to nephrectomy. In VHL, a planned approach to lesion management is important.
- **Metastasis-directed therapy.** For oligoprogression (limited sites of progression), SBRT can allow systemic therapy to continue without a switch.
- **Monitoring.** Track eGFR, proteinuria (urine protein-to-creatinine ratio), and BP on each cycle. Avoid mTOR-related hyperlipidemia and hyperglycemia as much as possible.
- **Early nephrology referral** if eGFR is declining, to plan for dialysis or transplant potential if needed.

## Q1: Belzutifan-induced anemia and ESA use

### Mechanism

Belzutifan inhibits HIF-2α, which normally drives erythropoietin (EPO) transcription in the kidney, mainly in interstitial fibroblast-like cells. Blocking HIF-2α lowers EPO production, which reduces erythropoiesis. This is an on-target effect, which fits the pattern you describe. Anemia is one of the most common belzutifan toxicities, and it is dose-related and often cumulative.

Her anemia is probably not purely drug-related. She has lupus nephritis with an eGFR around 35, so anemia of CKD is likely contributing. Before attributing all of it to belzutifan, I would check:

- **Hemolysis:** SLE can cause autoimmune hemolytic anemia. Check reticulocyte count, LDH, haptoglobin, bilirubin, peripheral smear, and a direct antiglobulin test (DAT).
- **Iron and nutritional status:** Ferritin, transferrin saturation, B12, and folate. Also check for occult GI or urinary blood loss.
- **Marrow effects:** Lupus-related cytopenias, drug effects, and infiltration are less likely but should be considered if the picture doesn't fit.
- **CKD contribution:** Reflected in her baseline anemia, if she had one before starting belzutifan.

### Management

- **Hold belzutifan** for Grade 3 anemia (Hb below 8 g/dL) and resume only after recovery, usually at a reduced dose. Confirm the exact hold and dose-reduction thresholds in the current prescribing information, because they have been updated over time.
- **Transfuse** for Hb 7.0 with symptoms. In a patient with active hypoxia and possible cardiac reserve issues, a restrictive threshold of about 7 to 8 g/dL is standard, but she is symptomatic, so transfusion is reasonable. Irradiated, leukoreduced products are typically used in patients who may be transplant candidates. Her SLE may also make transfusion-related alloimmunization more relevant, so type and screen carefully.
- **Iron** if deficient.

### Is an ESA permissible?

**I would not use an ESA here.** The reasons are:

1. **The boxed warning is about this exact scenario.** ESAs carry a boxed warning that they shortened survival and/or increased the risk of tumor progression or recurrence in patients with cancer. The labeled indication is anemia from myelosuppressive chemotherapy, with an explicit caveat against use when cure is the goal. Belzutifan-induced anemia is not chemotherapy-induced, so ESA use would be off-label, and the tumor-progression signal is especially relevant to a patient with active, growing renal disease.
2. **Thrombosis risk.** ESAs increase thromboembolic risk. SLE, especially with antiphospholipid antibodies, already raises that risk, and she also has hypoxia and possibly reduced mobility.
3. **Hypertension risk.** ESAs can raise blood pressure, which matters in lupus nephritis with reduced eGFR.
4. **Ineffective mechanism?** Belzutifan blocks the signal that makes EPO. Adding exogenous EPO would bypass that signal, so ESAs could be a rational add-on in theory. In practice, the safety concerns outweigh that.

If the anemia persists and transfusion dependence becomes a problem, the decision should go to hematology and oncology jointly, with documented informed consent. Even then, I would only use an ESA as a last resort with close monitoring for tumor progression.

## Q2: Surgical threshold and the right nephron-sparing approach

### The canonical threshold

In VHL-associated RCC, the commonly cited threshold for intervention is a solid renal mass reaching about **3 cm**, with growth rate also used as a trigger (rapid growth, roughly 5 mm per year or more, supports earlier intervention). The NIH-based VHL surveillance protocols and the Choyke-era data both support this approach. The aim is to intervene before metastatic potential develops while preserving nephrons.

Her lesion is 3.5 cm, already above the 3 cm threshold, and it grew 1.3 cm in the interval. That growth rate is a second reason to act. This is not a stable lesion under observation.

### Choosing the modality

Given a solitary kidney and eGFR of 35, nephron sparing is essential, and each option has a different risk profile:

- **Partial nephrectomy (preferred):** This is the standard for a 3.5 cm solid mass in a solitary kidney. Robotic or open PN with minimized warm ischemia time is preferred. Enucleation or tumor-sparing techniques can help preserve nephrons. Plan for surgery with a careful ischemia strategy, because ischemic injury in a solitary kidney with CKD can precipitate dialysis. Use a nephrometry score (RENAL or PADUA) for surgical planning, and consider cold ischemia or a clamp-free approach if the anatomy allows.
- **Ablation (cryoablation or RFA):** Generally favored for small tumors, usually under about 3 cm. At 3.5 cm, efficacy and local control are less certain, and repeated ablation may be needed. This is a less attractive option as a single definitive treatment here.
- **Active surveillance:** Not appropriate now, given the 3.5 cm size and growth.
- **Radical nephrectomy:** Avoid. In a solitary kidney, it would mean dialysis dependence or transplant.

### Sequencing with the systemic problem

- **Correct the anemia first.** Transfuse to a safer hemoglobin before surgery, and do not operate with an Hb of 7.
- **Hold belzutifan** perioperatively according to your anesthesia and surgical protocols, and discuss timing with oncology. Stopping belzutifan for surgery is reasonable because the drug has a long half-life and the surgical team needs a stable hemoglobin.
- **Evaluate the hypoxia before surgery.** Exclude pulmonary embolism, pneumonitis, effusion, and cardiac causes. Anemia contributes to hypoxia-related symptoms, but do not assume it is the only cause.
- **Lupus and thrombosis.** Check antiphospholipid antibodies if not already done, coordinate with rheumatology and nephrology, and plan periprocedural anticoagulation carefully.
- **Lupus activity.** Confirm that the nephritis is quiescent before major surgery.
- **Renal protection.** Nephrology should co-manage. Avoid nephrotoxins, and plan contrast and perioperative fluid management carefully.
